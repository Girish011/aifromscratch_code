Learning hierarchy for AI engineering

Learn vectors and the rest of ML foundations in the right order.

This page is designed as a mental map: start from the math objects, then move to learning algorithms, then connect each idea to the part of AI engineering where it actually shows up, such as embeddings, training loops, evaluation, retrieval, optimization, and numerical reliability.

Phase 01
Math foundations
Build intuition before formulas

1. Vectors, norms, distances

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, like position, features, or an embedding. Norms tell you “how big” a vector is, and distances tell you how far two vectors are from each other.

Think: one movie embedding vector compared to another movie embedding vector using cosine similarity.

Used in AI engineering

  • Embeddings for RAG, semantic search, recommender systems.
  • KNN, clustering, nearest-neighbor retrieval.
  • Feature scaling and similarity metrics.

2. Matrices and linear systems

A matrix is a table of numbers that usually represents many vectors together or a transformation applied to vectors. Linear systems ask: what values satisfy multiple equations at the same time?

Think: your dataset is often a matrix: rows are samples, columns are features.

Used in AI engineering

  • Design matrices in regression and classification.
  • Batched operations on GPUs.
  • Solving least-squares problems and calibration tasks.

3. Matrix transformations, eigenvalues, eigenvectors

A matrix can rotate, stretch, squash, or project vectors. Eigenvectors are special directions that stay on the same line after the transformation; eigenvalues tell you how much they scale.

Think: PCA finds important directions of variation in the data.

Used in AI engineering

  • PCA and covariance analysis.
  • Stability analysis for iterative methods.
  • Understanding dominant directions in data or operators.

4. Derivatives, gradients, chain rule, automatic differentiation

A derivative measures how much an output changes when an input changes. A gradient is the vector of derivatives for many parameters. The chain rule passes change information through a composition of functions, and autodiff automates that bookkeeping.

Think: loss depends on prediction, prediction depends on weights, so gradients flow backward from loss to weights.

Used in AI engineering

  • Backpropagation in neural networks.
  • Training custom losses and differentiable pipelines.
  • Frameworks like PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow autodiff engines.

5. Probability, distributions, Bayes, sampling, statistics

Probability models uncertainty; distributions describe how values are spread. Bayes updates belief after seeing evidence. Statistics helps you summarize, estimate, test, and reason from data rather than from anecdotes.

Think: spam probability changes after you observe words like “free”, “offer”, or “urgent”.

Used in AI engineering

  • Naive Bayes, uncertainty estimation, calibration.
  • Data analysis, A/B tests, confidence intervals.
  • Sampling training batches and synthetic generation steps.

6. Optimization, convexity, gradient descent family

Optimization is the problem of finding parameters that minimize loss. Convex problems are easier because every local minimum is global; deep learning is usually not convex, but gradient-based methods still work well in practice.

Think: SGD, Momentum, RMSProp, Adam are all different ways to update parameters using gradients.

Used in AI engineering

  • Training loops for regression, transformers, diffusion, vision models.
  • Learning-rate schedules, weight decay, convergence tuning.
  • Prompt tuning, fine-tuning, RL optimization variants.

7. SVD, PCA, dimensionality reduction

SVD factorizes a matrix into meaningful parts; PCA uses similar linear-algebra ideas to find lower-dimensional structure. t-SNE and UMAP are mainly for visualization, not for preserving every original distance perfectly.

Think: compress 10,000-dimensional features into a few principal directions for analysis.

Used in AI engineering

  • Feature compression and latent structure discovery.
  • Embedding visualization and error analysis.
  • Low-rank approximations and efficient adaptation ideas.

8. Tensors, numerical stability, information theory, Fourier, graphs, stochastic processes

Tensors generalize vectors and matrices to higher dimensions. Numerical stability asks whether your computations break due to floating-point issues. Entropy and KL divergence measure uncertainty and distribution mismatch. Fourier transforms move between time/space and frequency views. Graph theory models nodes and relations. Stochastic processes model systems evolving with randomness.

Think: a batch of RGB images is a 4D tensor; softmax overflow is a numerical stability problem.

Used in AI engineering

  • Deep learning arrays, attention tensors, convolution inputs.
  • Stable softmax, log-sum-exp, mixed precision training.
  • Language modeling loss, diffusion noise schedules, graph neural networks, signal models.
Phase 02
ML fundamentals
Turn math into predictive systems

9. What is machine learning

Machine learning is the idea of learning patterns from data instead of hard-coding every rule. You define inputs, targets, a model family, a loss, and an update procedure.

Template: data → model → prediction → loss → gradient → parameter update.

Used in AI engineering

  • Framing product problems as prediction, ranking, generation, or decision tasks.
  • Choosing whether a problem needs heuristics, classical ML, or deep learning.

10. Linear regression and logistic regression

Linear regression predicts continuous numbers; logistic regression predicts class probabilities. These are the cleanest places to understand parameters, features, loss, gradients, regularization, and decision boundaries.

Think: price prediction versus spam/not-spam classification.

Used in AI engineering

  • Baselines that are cheap, fast, and interpretable.
  • Binary classifiers, conversion models, fraud risk scores.
  • Debugging data pipelines before using bigger models.

11. Trees, random forests, boosting

Trees split data by feature rules. Random forests average many trees; boosting builds sequential weak learners that focus on previous errors.

Think: “if income < X and usage > Y, then churn risk increases.”

Used in AI engineering

  • Tabular prediction tasks with mixed feature types.
  • Feature importance, business-rule alignment, strong baseline models.
  • Structured-data systems where deep nets may be unnecessary.

12. SVM, KNN, distance metrics, Naive Bayes

These models teach different modeling biases: max-margin thinking with SVM, local-neighbor reasoning with KNN, probabilistic independence assumptions with Naive Bayes.

Think: KNN depends heavily on distance choice and feature scaling.

Used in AI engineering

  • Text classification baselines and anomaly heuristics.
  • Similarity search intuition that later helps with embeddings and vector DBs.
  • Small-data classification problems.

13. Clustering, anomaly detection, time series

Clustering groups similar items without labels. Anomaly detection finds unusual behavior. Time series adds order, trend, seasonality, and temporal dependence.

Think: grouping customers, flagging fraud spikes, forecasting traffic.

Used in AI engineering

  • User segmentation, observability, fraud and abuse detection.
  • Infra demand forecasting and capacity planning.
  • Monitoring embedding drift or model behavior drift.

14. Feature engineering, selection, imbalance handling

Good features often matter more than fancy models. Feature selection removes noisy variables; imbalance handling prevents rare classes from being ignored.

Think: log transforms, counts, recency features, class weights, SMOTE.

Used in AI engineering

  • Improving tabular models and classical ML pipelines.
  • Making evaluation fair when positives are rare.
  • Reducing overfitting and training cost.

15. Evaluation, bias-variance, hyperparameter tuning, pipelines

Evaluation tells you whether the model is truly useful, not just accurate on one split. Bias-variance explains underfitting versus overfitting. Pipelines make preprocessing and training reproducible; experiment tracking makes comparisons trustworthy.

Think: use precision/recall for imbalance, not raw accuracy alone.

Used in AI engineering

  • Reliable offline evaluation before deployment.
  • Cross-validation, ablations, hyperparameter search.
  • MLOps, experiment tracking, and reproducible production systems.

Core concepts in plain English

These mirror the style of your epoch/loss example: short, direct, and tied to training intuition.

Vector

An ordered list of numbers representing one thing. In AI, that “thing” may be a user, a token, an image patch, or a retrieved document chunk.

Matrix

A grid of numbers representing many vectors together or a transformation applied to them. In practice, your batch data and learned weights are often matrices.

Gradient

A direction telling parameters how to change so the loss decreases fastest locally. Training uses this signal to update weights.

Eigenvalue

A scaling factor for a special direction of a transformation. It helps explain dominant modes in data and matrix behavior.

Entropy

A measure of uncertainty. High entropy means spread-out uncertainty; low entropy means confidence is concentrated.

Pipeline

The full repeatable path from raw data to trained model to evaluation. Good pipelines reduce leakage, bugs, and irreproducible results.
